# Stage 1: Past research behind the protocol, the question and the groups

Three kinds of source support Stage 1: two methods papers that give the
groups for any research question, methods sources on how to form a qualitative
research question, and serendipity research whose models match the groups. Section names are given so a reader can find the place in
each source. All serendipity papers listed are in `papers/pdf/`; the methods
papers are in the literature library. Publication details were checked against each paper.

## A0. The general frame behind the groups (any research question)

The ten groups are a general template (`protocol.md`) filled in for
serendipity. The template rests on two methods papers already in the
literature library:

| Source | What it says | Groups it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Podsakoff, MacKenzie and Podsakoff (2016), *Recommendations for creating better concept definitions*, Organizational Research Methods | Define a concept by its attributes and say which are necessary; describe its dimensions; show how it differs from related concepts; clarify its meaning by specifying some antecedents and consequences, without defining it by them alone (stages 2 to 4) | definition, type, rival; trigger, background; outcome |
| Langley (1999), *Strategies for theorizing from process data*, Academy of Management Review | Process data are stories of what happened and who did what when: events, activities and choices ordered over time, explained as a sequence leading to an outcome ("Data composed of events") | trigger, mechanism, role, stage marker; account |

**condition** is a design choice: the circumstances that make the chain more
or less likely. For serendipity it is grounded in Busch (2024) and the other
sources in section B.

## B. The frame of groups

### Busch (2024), the closest match

*Towards a theory of serendipity: A systematic review and conceptualization*,
Journal of Management Studies, doi:10.1111/joms.12890. A systematic review of
management research on serendipity. Its model maps onto the groups almost
one for one:

| In Busch (2024) | Section | Group |
|---|---|---|
| Definition: "surprising discovery that results from unplanned moments in which our decisions and actions lead to valuable outcomes", built on three necessary conditions: agency, surprise, value | Necessary conditions | definition |
| A serendipity trigger is spotted | Figure 3 and text around it | trigger |
| Association: relating the unexpected to a goal ("connecting the dots") | Figure 3 | mechanism |
| Materialization: latent value made real | Figure 3 | role, outcome |
| Detection and linking qualities (alertness, curiosity, intuition) | Individual-level catalysts | background |
| Individual and organizational enablers and inhibitors (for example self-censoring) | Antecedents of (cultivating) serendipity | condition |
| Types, by the actor's initial intent and by the outcome | Types of serendipity | type |
| Distinguished from luck and from targeted innovation | Necessary conditions | rival |

Busch also notes that trigger and association can happen at once or be
drawn out over time, which is why the chain in Stage 1 is a reading aid
rather than a fixed order.

## C. What the sources suggest for Stage 1

1. **Definition:** Busch's three conditions (agency, surprise, value) and the
   core shared by Yaqub, de Rond, Cunha and Dew are the same idea in
   different words. They give the narrow definition.
2. **The chain is supported but not fixed:** Busch's trigger, association
   and materialization match background → trigger → mechanism → outcome.
3. **Stage matters:** Mirvahedi and Morrish's finding about early firm
   formation supports adding a stage marker for SQ2.
4. **Account is evidence:** three sources say people retell serendipity in
   biased ways, which supports keeping account as its own sub-question.
5. **Two studies used interviews with founders** (McFarlane and Carter;
   Reijonen and Heroum), so coding founders' own accounts has precedent.
