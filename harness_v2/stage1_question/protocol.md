# Stage 1 protocol: from a research question to sub-questions and analysis groups

A general set of instructions for any qualitative study that codes text. It
turns one research question into sub-questions, and each sub-question into
the analysis questions (codebook groups) a coder asks of every piece of data.
`research_question.md` applies it to this study.

Every source cited here was read in full. Each is in the literature library
or the project's papers folder.

## Three kinds of question

Keep them apart (Staller 2022):

- **Research question**: what the study as a whole answers.
- **Sub-questions**: the parts of it, each answerable on its own.
- **Analysis questions**: what a coder asks of each piece of data. In this
  harness these are the codebook groups.

Interview questions are a fourth kind; they do not arise here because the
interviews already exist.

## The six steps

### 1. Classify the question and choose the lenses

Three classifications and a choice of lenses, each recorded.

**Type.** The type decides the shape of the sub-questions. The
best-established distinction is process versus variance (Langley 1999):
variance questions ask about differences ("Does…", "How much…"), process
questions ask how things happen, and qualitative research is better suited to
the second (Maxwell 2009).

| Type | Typical form | Sub-questions ask about | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | What is happening? What patterns or kinds are there? | Instances, features, a typology | (our synthesis) |
| Conceptual | What is X? | Attributes, kinds, boundaries, look-alikes | Podsakoff, MacKenzie and Podsakoff (2016) |
| Process | How does X unfold, or shape P? | Events, actions, sequence, outcomes, timing | Langley (1999); Langley et al. (2013); Van de Ven and Engleman (2004) |
| Variance | What explains differences in Y? | Factors, how they relate, what strengthens or weakens the link | Langley (1999); Van de Ven and Engleman (2004) |
| Interpretive | How do people make sense of X? | Meanings, accounts, differences between actors | Welch et al. (2011) |
| Comparative | How does X differ across groups, settings or times? | The same sub-questions per group, then compared | Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007) |
| Developmental | How could X be done or supported? | Requirements, designs, what works | (our synthesis) |

A question can mix types; record the main one and any secondary ones. This
list is our synthesis from the sources named, not an established typology.

**Lenses.** A lens is a way of breaking the question into parts. Choose one
or more to fit the type:

| Lens | Breaks the question into | Fits | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concept anatomy | Definition, kinds, scope conditions, relation to similar concepts, antecedents, consequences | Conceptual questions; the "what counts" part of any question | Podsakoff et al. (2016); Suddaby (2010) |
| Event sequence | Events, activities and choices, their order, the outcome | Process | Langley (1999) |
| Process motor | The pattern that drives change: life cycle, goal-seeking, conflict, or variation and selection | Process, when asking why it unfolds as it does | Van de Ven (1992) |
| Variables | Factors, links, what strengthens or weakens the links | Variance | Langley (1999) |
| Question words | What, how, why; who, where, when | Any type, as a completeness check | Whetten (1989) |
| Question elements | Emphasis, sample, phenomenon of interest, context | Any type, to check the question is fully specified | (our synthesis) |
| Levels | Individual, team, organisation, environment | Multi-level questions | Zilber and Meyer (2022) |
| Time | Before, during, after; when a process begins and ends | Process and comparative questions | McMullen and Dimov (2013); Lévesque and Stephan (2020) |

**State of theory.** Nascent, intermediate or mature (Edmondson and McManus
2007). Nascent theory calls for few, open sub-questions; intermediate theory
for sub-questions that use existing constructs and stay open to new ones;
mature theory for hypotheses.

**Deductive, inductive or abductive.** A deductive study states its
constructs in advance and asks questions rather than testing hypotheses
(Robinson and Bailey-Rodriguez 2025). An inductive study lets concepts come
from the data (Gioia et al. 2013). An abductive study starts from existing
theory and treats surprises in the data as the occasion for new theory
(Timmermans and Tavory 2012; Sætre and Van de Ven 2021); abductive coding
applies codes from theory and adds codes where the data do not fit
(Vila-Henninger et al. 2024). Whatever the choice, the method of analysis has
to fit the question (Gehman et al. 2018).

### 2. Take the question apart

Write down each part of the question and of the data:

- the **construct** (what is studied);
- the **relation** (the verb: shapes, explains, means);
- the **process** and its **span** (from when to when);
- the **actors** (who);
- the **setting** (where);
- the **data**: what kind, and its time orientation (retrospective accounts,
  observation in real time, documents).

A complete theory says what, how and why, and who, where and when
(Whetten 1989). Level of analysis, unit of analysis and time orientation
should fit the question (Zilber and Meyer 2022).

### 3. Apply the lenses to get the sub-questions

Each lens gives a standard set of sub-questions. For a **process** question
about X in process P, using concept anatomy for what counts and event
sequence for the rest:

| Standard sub-question | Grounding |
|---|---|
| What counts as X? | Attributes, kinds, look-alikes (Podsakoff et al. 2016) |
| Where in P does it happen, and what sets it off? | Antecedents (Podsakoff et al.); events and timing (Langley 1999); stages of a venture (Bhave 1994; Fisher, Kotha and Lahiri 2016; McMullen and Dimov 2013) |
| What do actors bring and do? | Antecedents the actor brings (Podsakoff et al.); activities and choices (Langley 1999) |
| What does it change? | Consequences (Podsakoff et al.); the outcome a sequence leads to (Langley 1999) |
| When is it more or less likely? | Scope conditions, the circumstances in which a construct applies (Suddaby 2010), extended to what makes it more or less likely; grounded in research on X |
| How is it told? | Needed when the data are retrospective accounts, whose accuracy can be judged and improved (Huber and Power 1985; Golden 1992; Miller, Cardinal and Glick 1997) |

Use the question-words lens to check nothing is missing. For a
**conceptual** question: what counts as X, what kinds there are, and what X
is not. For a **variance** question: which factors go with Y, how they
relate, and what strengthens or weakens the link. For an **interpretive**
question: what X means to the actors, how they talk about it, and how
meanings differ. For a **comparative** question, ask the sub-questions of
each group and add one: how do the answers differ?

### 4. Anchor every sub-question

Each sub-question must trace to a part of the question or of the data from
step 2. A sub-question with no anchor is dropped; a part with no
sub-question is a gap to fill. This keeps the design coherent: every
component connects to the others (Maxwell 2009).

### 5. Test every sub-question

Keep a sub-question only if it passes all four tests:

Keep the set small: include every factor that matters and leave out those
that add little (Whetten 1989, on comprehensiveness and parsimony).

1. **Answerable from the data**: a coder could find evidence for it in the
   material (Maxwell 2009).
2. **Serves the central question**: answering it helps answer the whole.
3. **Distinct**: two coders would not put the same material under it and
   another sub-question.
4. **Open**: it asks what or how, not yes or no (Agee 2009).

### 6. Turn sub-questions into analysis groups, and record the trail

For each sub-question, write the one to three analysis questions a coder asks
of each piece of data. Each becomes a codebook group. Add one more group,
**misfit**, for material that bears on the research question but that no
other code captures. It has one code per group it can overflow from, plus
one for material no group asks about. Each misfit records a short label, the
data it rests on, and why no existing code fits. This is what lets an
abductive design change the codebook: one generic code for what no code fits
(MacQueen et al. 1998), a separate look at the evidence a framework cannot
hold (Carroll et al. 2013), and codes added where the data do not fit
(Vila-Henninger et al. 2024). Record the
classification, the parts, the anchors, the test results, and anything
dropped or merged, with reasons. Mark the record DRAFT, agree it, mark it
AGREED, and commit. The record is what a reader or editor checks.

## The analysis groups for a process question

| Standard sub-question | Group | Analysis question asked of each episode |
|---|---|---|
| What counts as X? | definition | Does this episode count as X at all? |
| | type | What kind of X is it? |
| | rival | What else could explain it? |
| Where does it happen, and what sets it off? | trigger | What event started it? |
| | (stage marker) | Where in P does it fall? |
| What do actors bring and do? | background | What did the actor already have? |
| | mechanism | What did the actor do with it? |
| What does it change? | role | What part did it play in P? |
| | outcome | What came of it? |
| When is it more or less likely? | condition | What made it easier or harder? |
| How is it told? | account | How does the actor narrate it afterwards? |
| (all) | misfit | What in this episode does no other code capture? |

The unit is the **episode**: one stretch of the data in which X happens and
someone does something with it. The groups fit as a chain, background →
trigger → mechanism → role and outcome, with condition shaping the whole;
definition, type and rival deciding whether and how the episode counts; and
account describing how it was told. The order is a reading aid, not a rule.
