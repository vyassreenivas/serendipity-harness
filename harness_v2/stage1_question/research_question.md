This file applies the Stage 1 protocol (`protocol.md`) to this study,
following its six steps. X is serendipity; P is the entrepreneurial process.

## Central question

How does serendipity shape the entrepreneurial process, from the first idea to
the developing venture?

## Unit of analysis

An **episode**: one stretch of a founder's account in which something
unexpected happens and the founder does something with it. One interview can
contain several episodes, or none.

## What each group looks for

The "in an interview" lines are made-up illustrations of what a founder might
say. They are not quotes from the Starter Story data.

1. **definition**: the conditions an episode must meet to count. The
   anchor is Busch (2024), who defines serendipity as "surprising discovery
   that results from unplanned moments in which our decisions and actions
   lead to valuable outcomes", resting on three necessary conditions:
   agency, surprise and value. These are the same core the four definitional
   papers share: something unexpected, something of value, and a founder who
   recognised it and acted. These codes decide the two verdicts on each
   episode (narrow and broad); see open point 3.
   *In an interview:* "I wasn't looking for this at all, but I saw it could
   work and went for it."
2. **type**: the kind of serendipity, for example finding something other
   than what was sought, or reaching the original goal by a surprising route.
   *In an interview:* "We set out to build a tool for agencies and ended up
   selling to schools."
3. **rival**: look-alikes that are not serendipity: systematic search, pure
   luck, a plan carried out. A "supported" verdict names the rivals the
   account also fits, or says none is plausible.
   *In an interview:* "We researched 40 niches and picked the best one."
4. **trigger**: the unexpected event: an odd observation, a chance
   conversation, a mistake, even an unlucky event.
   *In an interview:* "A customer used it completely wrong, and that's where
   the idea came from."
5. **background**: what the founder brought before it happened: knowledge,
   experience, contacts, a search already under way.
   *In an interview:* "I'd spent ten years in logistics, so I knew straight
   away what that meant."
6. **mechanism**: what the founder did between the trigger and the result:
   noticing, connecting, judging, acting.
   *In an interview:* "I put the two together, built a landing page that
   weekend, and it took off."
7. **role**: the part it played in the venture: starting it, redirecting it,
   or advancing it.
   *In an interview:* "We pivoted the whole business after that."
8. **outcome**: what came of it: a product, a market, revenue; or nothing,
   if it was dropped or forgotten.
   *In an interview:* "That channel is now 60% of our revenue."
9. **condition**: what made it easier or harder to notice or pursue:
   flexibility, slack, openness, or their absence.
   *In an interview:* "We hadn't raised money yet, so we could change
   direction overnight."
10. **account**: how the founder tells it: as luck, as planned, as their own
    insight, or tidied in hindsight. The interviews are retrospective
    stories, so how serendipity is told is part of the evidence.
    *In an interview:* "Honestly, we just got lucky."
11. **misfit**: anything in the episode that bears on the research question
    but that no code in groups 2 to 10 captures. It answers no single
    sub-question; it is how the abductive design lets the data change the
    codebook. See "The misfit group" below.
    *In an interview:* "Then the city changed its zoning rules, and suddenly
    our site was worth three times as much." (no trigger code covers a policy
    change)

## The misfit group

Each misfit code names the group the material overflows from. Definition has
no misfit code: an episode that may or may not count as serendipity gets the
verdict **unresolved**.

| Code | Use it when | Do not use it when |
|---|---|---|
| MIS-TYP | The episode is serendipity, but no type code describes it | A type fits roughly: use it and note the doubt |
| MIS-RIV | The account fits another explanation that is not a rival code | The founder only denies chance: that is account |
| MIS-TRG | Something unexpected started it, but no trigger code names that kind of event | The trigger is unclear: leave trigger blank |
| MIS-BKG | The founder brought something relevant that no background code covers | It is a circumstance, not something the founder had: that is condition |
| MIS-MEC | The founder did something with the event that no mechanism code covers | |
| MIS-ROL | It played a part in the venture other than starting, redirecting or advancing it | |
| MIS-OUT | A result that no outcome code covers | |
| MIS-CND | A circumstance that made it easier or harder, not covered by a condition code | |
| MIS-ACC | A way of telling it that no account code covers | |
| MIS-NEW | Relevant to the research question, but no group asks about it (for example, a co-founder rather than the founder saw the opportunity) | It belongs to a group: use that group's misfit code |

Every misfit code records three things: a short label in the coder's words,
the paragraphs it rests on, and one line on why no existing code fits.

Misfits are handled in two steps, both by rule:

1. **Capture during coding, with no threshold.** Every coder, person or
   model, records every misfit as it comes, however rare. Nothing is filtered
   out at this step, because coding only a sample of the text tends to lose
   the text no code captures (MacQueen et al. 1998).
2. **Abductive analysis at the end of each calibration round.** The
   researchers pool the round's misfit labels, compare them across coders,
   and ask what each cluster means for the research question (Timmermans and
   Tavory 2012; Vila-Henninger et al. 2024). This follows Carroll et al.
   (2013), who code against the framework first and then analyse separately
   the evidence it cannot hold.

A misfit label becomes a code in the next codebook version in one of two
ways, both recorded in the commit that adds the code:

- **Recurrence:** the label appears in at least a set share of the round's
  interviews and is raised by at least two coders. For example, 10% of the
  round's interviews; the share is to be set before coding (open point 4).
- **A written theoretical reason:** a label seen only once can still become a
  code if the researchers write down why it matters for the research
  question, for example because it matches a construct in the literature.

A misfit code used often signals that its group needs revision. The design
follows MacQueen et al. (1998), who suggest one generic code for responses no
code fits; Carroll et al. (2013), who analyse separately the evidence a
framework cannot hold; and Vila-Henninger et al. (2024), whose abductive
coding adds codes where the data do not fit.

## Calibration rounds, the held-out test and scale-up

Agreed in discussion on 16 October 2026. The design rules are fixed here; the
thresholds are left open for Vyas and Ravi to set before coding (open point
6). The steps follow MacQueen et al. (1998): coders code the same sample
independently, the results are compared, the codebook is revised, and
agreement is checked again, against criteria decided in advance.

1. **Calibration rounds of 20 to 30 interviews.** In each round the
   researchers and the model code the same fresh sample of interviews with
   the current codebook version. The codebook is frozen within a round. At
   the end of the round the coders are compared, the misfits are analysed
   (above), and the codebook is revised into the next version.
2. **Stop when all four conditions hold:**
   - the codebook is stable: the round produced no new code and no change to
     what a code means. Clarifying when to use a code does not count as a
     change (see "How stability is measured" below);
   - the researchers agree with each other at or above a set level;
   - the model's agreement with each researcher is within a set margin of the
     researchers' agreement with each other;
   - misfits are few, and they form no new cluster.
3. **A budget cap, not a target.** The rounds stop when the conditions hold,
   not after a set number. As a limit on time and people, calibration stops
   after about 150 interviews (about five rounds) even if the codebook is
   still changing; the researchers then write down why, and freeze the
   codebook as it stands. This cap is about four times the 36 interviews
   after which Guest et al. (2006) saw no further definition changes.
4. **A held-out test.** The frozen codebook is tested on 30 to 50 interviews
   that no round has used, coded by the researchers and the model. The test
   checks that agreement holds on new material, not only on the material the
   codebook was revised on.
5. **Scale-up.** The model codes the rest of the corpus with the frozen
   codebook. The researchers spot-check about 5% of the model's coding.
6. **End-of-corpus misfit analysis.** The misfits the model recorded across
   the corpus are pooled and coded abductively by the researchers. The result
   is findings, revised codes, or rarely a targeted recode of the interviews
   a revised code affects.
7. **No loop over the full corpus.** The codebook is not revised and the
   whole corpus recoded again. Revision happens in the calibration rounds;
   after the held-out test, what the codebook cannot hold is analysed as
   misfit material instead.

The shape is a funnel: the model codes the whole corpus deductively, and
people analyse the narrow residue the codebook cannot hold.

### How stability is measured, and why rounds are not counted in advance

Two studies measured when a codebook stops changing. Guest, Bunce and Johnson
(2006) coded 60 interviews in ten cumulative rounds of six. 92% of the codes
for the first site existed after 12 interviews, and 58% of all definition
changes had been made by then; the last change came at 36 interviews. Of the
36 changes, 28 (78%) only clarified when to use a code. Hennink, Kaiser and
Marconi (2017) reached code saturation at 9 of 25 interviews: 91% of codes
and 92% of definition changes. But conceptual codes needed 16 to 24
interviews, or more, before no new dimensions of their meaning appeared.
Neither study fixes a number of rounds; both measure when change stops.
In management research, Eisenhardt (1989) gives the same rule for theory
building: stop iterating between theory and data when the improvement to
theory becomes minimal, and accept that this point is set in practice
alongside time and money. That is the reason for a budget cap.

So the harness records, for every round:

- the number of new codes;
- the number of changes to what a code means, kept apart from clarifications
  of when to use it (Guest et al.'s distinction);
- agreement between the researchers, and between the model and each of them.

The codebook freezes after the first round with no new code and no change
of meaning, once agreement holds. The cumulative counts are reported as
curves, as both studies do, so a reader can see where the codebook settled.

Three differences from those studies are expected to pull in opposite
directions. The codebook starts from the literature, and deductive codes
settled early in Hennink et al. The founders and their ventures are far more
varied than either study's sample, and serendipity codes are conceptual, both
of which Hennink et al. found need more interviews. We therefore expect the
codebook to settle within two or three rounds (40 to 90 interviews), and set
the cap well above that.

Freezing needs code saturation only: every code exists and is applied
consistently. Meaning saturation, a full understanding of each code
(Hennink et al. 2017), is reached afterwards, when every code has many
coded passages across the corpus to analyse, and through the misfit
analysis.

## How the groups fit together

One episode, read in order:

    background  ->  trigger  ->  mechanism  ->  role and outcome
    (what the       (the          (what they     (what it changed)
     founder had)    unexpected    did)
                     event)

- **condition** shapes the whole chain.
- **definition**, **type** and **rival** decide whether the episode counts,
  and how.
- **account** describes how the episode was told.
- **misfit** holds whatever the other groups miss.

The order is a reading aid, not a rule. A trigger and the founder's response
can happen at once, or the response can come long after the trigger.

## Open points

1. **Stage marker for SQ2.** SQ2 asks *where in the process* serendipity
   happens, which needs a marker for the venture's stage (first idea,
   founding, first customers, growth, change of direction). It is not yet a
   group. Mirvahedi and Morrish (2017) found serendipity concentrated in early
   firm formation, which this marker would let us test.
2. **Levels lens.** Busch (2024) works at several levels. Applying the levels
   lens would add sub-questions about the venture team, the organisation and
   the environment, not only the founder. Not adopted yet; to decide.
3. **Narrow and broad verdicts.** One option: the broad verdict uses Busch's
   (2023) three conditions (agency, surprise, value); the narrow verdict adds
   Dew's (2009) condition that the founder was already searching. To decide.

4. **The recurrence threshold for promoting a misfit.** The rule is fixed
   (see "The misfit group"): recurrence, or a written theoretical reason. The
   share of a round's interviews that counts as recurrence is open; one
   suggestion is 10%, raised by at least two coders. To set before coding.
5. **Code prefixes.** The July codebook used BND, close to condition, and LOC,
   whose meaning needs checking. This record uses BKG for background, ROL for
   role and CND for condition. To confirm.
6. **The thresholds for calibration and scale-up.** The rules are fixed (see
   "Calibration rounds, the held-out test and scale-up"); the numbers are
   open: the agreement level the researchers must reach, the margin the model
   must meet, what counts as "few" misfits, the round size within 20 to 30,
   the interview budget for calibration (about 150), the held-out size
   within 30 to 50, and the
   spot-check share (about 5%). To set before the first round.

## Decided

- **Account stays its own sub-question (SQ6)**, decided 16 October 2026.
  The interviews are success stories told after the fact to a publishing
  platform, so how serendipity is told must be coded to separate what
  happened from how it is told. People play serendipity down (Busch and
  Grimes 2023) or use it to make a good story (Dew 2009). Brownell et al.
  (2024) studied how 70 founders talk about luck in *How I Built This*
  interviews, a close precedent for this data. How founders tell it is also
  a finding in its own right.
  Maxwell (2009) separates realist questions (what happened) from
  instrumentalist ones (what people report happened); the data are reports,
  so SQ1 to SQ5 ask the first kind and SQ6 the second.
- **Misfits form their own group (11)**, decided 16 October 2026, rather than
  an "other" code inside each group. Coders look in one place, and the
  review between rounds covers one group.
- **Misfits in two steps**, agreed 16 October 2026: captured during coding
  with no threshold, and analysed abductively at the end of each calibration
  round; promoted to a code by recurrence or a written theoretical reason.
- **Calibration, held-out test and scale-up**, agreed 16 October 2026: rounds
  of 20 to 30 interviews until four stopping conditions hold, a held-out test, model scale-up with spot checks, and
  end-of-corpus misfit analysis, with no loop over the full corpus.
- **Stopping rule based on evidence**, decided 17 October 2026: calibration
  stops after the first round with no new code and no change of meaning,
  once agreement holds (Guest et al. 2006; Hennink et al. 2017). The earlier
  fixed cap of about five rounds becomes a budget limit of about 150
  interviews.
